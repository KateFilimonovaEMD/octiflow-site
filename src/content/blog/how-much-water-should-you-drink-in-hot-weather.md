“Drink more water” is technically correct advice for a hot day. It just isn’t very useful.

More than what? How often? Does everyone need electrolytes? And how do you know when you have had enough?

The honest answer is that temperature alone cannot give you a personal water target. A quiet afternoon indoors is very different from walking through a city in direct sun, working outside or exercising in high humidity.

Your fluid needs depend on what your body is actually losing.

## There is no universal summer number

The European Food Safety Authority sets adequate total water intakes of **2.0 litres per day for adult women and 2.5 litres for adult men**.

But those numbers are often misunderstood.

They refer to **total water**, including drinking water, other beverages and moisture from food. They also apply to moderate temperatures and moderate physical activity. EFSA describes them as population reference values, not precise prescriptions for every individual.

Hot weather, exercise, illness, pregnancy, breastfeeding, body size and some medications can all change how much fluid a person needs.

So the usual daily number can be a starting point, but it is not a fixed summer limit or a target you should force yourself to hit exactly.

## Why heat changes your water needs

Sweating is one of the main ways your body releases heat. As sweat evaporates from your skin, it helps cool you down.

At the same time, you lose water and electrolytes, particularly sodium.

How quickly that happens depends on more than the temperature shown in a weather app. Humidity, wind, direct sunlight, clothing, physical activity and how accustomed you are to the heat all affect your body’s ability to cool itself.

High humidity is especially uncomfortable because sweat evaporates less efficiently. You may continue sweating without getting the same cooling effect.

This is why two people can spend an hour outside in the same temperature and lose very different amounts of fluid. It is also why a universal formula based only on air temperature would be misleading.

## A practical guide for different kinds of hot days

You do not need to calculate every drop of sweat. It is more useful to match your drinking pattern to what you are doing.

### Mostly indoors, with little physical activity

Keep water available and drink regularly throughout the day. There is usually no reason to force several extra litres simply because it is hot outside, especially when you are spending most of the day in a cooled indoor space.

Pay attention to thirst, urine colour and how often you urinate.

### Walking, commuting or spending short periods outside

Carry water instead of assuming you will find it later. Drink before a longer trip outdoors and again when you return.

Take regular sips when you are sweating, rather than waiting until you feel exhausted and then drinking a large amount at once.

### Exercising or doing physical work in the heat

For **moderate activity in the heat lasting less than two hours**, the US National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health recommends about **one cup of water, or 240 ml, every 15 to 20 minutes**.

This is occupational heat guidance, not a compulsory target for everyone sitting outside on a warm day. Activity level and sweating still matter.

NIOSH also advises that fluid intake should generally not exceed **six cups per hour**, approximately 1.4 litres. Drinking excessive amounts in a short period is not safer and can, in rare cases, dangerously dilute sodium in the blood.

### Sweating for several hours

When heavy sweating continues for several hours, NIOSH recommends drinks containing balanced electrolytes.

That does not mean everyone needs a sports drink during summer. For an ordinary day without prolonged heavy sweating, official guidance still centres on water. Electrolyte drinks become more relevant during extended physical work, endurance exercise or other situations involving sustained sweat loss.

## Do you need electrolytes?

Usually, not just because the weather is hot.

Electrolytes are minerals with an electric charge, including sodium and potassium. They help regulate fluid balance and support nerve and muscle function.

You lose some electrolytes through sweat, but the amount varies widely. A person taking a short walk does not have the same replacement needs as someone working outdoors for several hours.

Sports drinks can be useful when sweating is prolonged, but they may also contain substantial amounts of sugar and sodium. They should not automatically replace water throughout the day.

A simple rule is:

- **Normal daily activity:** water is usually the main drink you need.
- **Several hours of sweating:** a balanced electrolyte drink may be useful.
- **A medical condition affecting fluid or electrolytes:** follow advice from a healthcare professional rather than a general hydration rule.

The World Health Organization also recommends drinking water regularly during hot weather while limiting alcoholic and sugary drinks.

## How to tell whether you are drinking enough

No home check can measure hydration perfectly, but a few signs are genuinely useful.

### Look at your urine

The CDC notes that light yellow or clear urine usually suggests you are drinking enough water.

Dark urine or urinating less often can be signs that you need more fluid. Urine colour can also be affected by medicines, supplements and health conditions, so it should be treated as a practical clue rather than a diagnosis.

### Notice other symptoms

Common signs of dehydration in adults include:

- thirst
- dry mouth
- dark-coloured urine
- urinating less than usual
- tiredness
- dizziness

Headache, weakness, nausea, muscle cramps and unusually heavy sweating can also appear when the body is struggling with heat.

### Use thirst, but understand its limits

For most healthy adults in ordinary conditions, thirst is a useful guide. The National Academies notes that normal drinking behaviour and thirst are generally enough to maintain hydration for the average person.

However, thirst may be less reliable in older adults, and it may not be enough on its own during prolonged exercise, illness or sustained heat exposure. Regular access to water is especially important in those situations.

## Water cannot make unsafe heat exposure safe

Hydration matters, but it is only one part of heat safety.

Drinking enough water does not cancel out hours in direct sun, intense exercise at the hottest time of day or a room that is dangerously hot.

The WHO recommends reducing strenuous activity during the hottest part of the day, staying in the shade, taking cooling breaks and spending time in a cool place when possible.

## Know the difference between dehydration and heat illness

Heat exhaustion and heat stroke need more than another glass of water.

### Possible heat exhaustion

Warning signs can include:

- heavy sweating
- weakness or unusual tiredness
- dizziness or fainting
- nausea or vomiting
- muscle cramps
- cool, pale or clammy skin

Move to a cool place, loosen unnecessary clothing, cool the skin with wet cloths or a cool bath and sip water.

Seek medical help if the person is vomiting, symptoms are getting worse or they continue for more than one hour.

### Possible heat stroke

Confusion, loss of consciousness, seizures and very high body temperature can indicate heat stroke.

Heat stroke is a medical emergency. Call emergency services immediately and begin cooling the person. Do not try to make an unconscious or confused person drink.

## Who needs to be especially careful?

Anyone can become ill in hot weather, but the risk is higher for:

- adults over 65
- infants and young children
- pregnant people
- people working or exercising outdoors
- people with heart, kidney, respiratory or other chronic conditions
- people taking medicines that affect sweating, urination, blood pressure or temperature regulation

Some older adults experience a reduced sense of thirst. Certain medications can also increase dehydration risk or make it harder for the body to cool itself.

Do not stop or change medication because of hot weather without speaking to a doctor or pharmacist.

People with heart failure, kidney disease or a prescribed fluid restriction should not simply increase their intake based on a general article. Both dehydration and excess fluid can be harmful in these conditions. A healthcare professional can help create a safe plan for hot days.

## A simple hot-weather hydration plan

You do not need a complicated formula.

1. Keep water within reach.
2. Drink regularly instead of trying to catch up late in the day.
3. Increase your intake when you are sweating or physically active.
4. Consider electrolytes when sweating continues for several hours.
5. Use urine colour, thirst and other symptoms as practical feedback.
6. Take cooling breaks and reduce activity when the heat becomes difficult.
7. Treat confusion, fainting or loss of consciousness as an emergency.

The right amount is not the largest amount you can drink. It is enough to replace your losses without forcing excessive fluid in a short period.
