Electrolyte drinks are everywhere. Powders, tablets and brightly coloured bottles promise better hydration after exercise, heat, travel or simply a busy day.

That can make plain water look incomplete. It is not.

Electrolytes are essential minerals, but most healthy people do not need a special electrolyte product during an ordinary day. [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/fluidandelectrolytebalance.html) explains that electrolytes come from the foods and fluids we consume, while CDC/NIOSH guidance says that regular meals and adequate water are generally enough to maintain water and electrolyte balance during normal activity, including much work in the heat.

Electrolyte replacement becomes more useful when the losses become unusually large, especially during prolonged heavy sweating or gastrointestinal illness.

The practical question is not whether electrolytes are "good". It is whether you are losing enough water and salts for replacing them separately to be useful.

## What are electrolytes?

Electrolytes are minerals that carry an electrical charge when dissolved in water or body fluids.

Important electrolytes include:

- sodium;
- potassium;
- chloride;
- magnesium;
- calcium;
- phosphate;
- bicarbonate.

They help regulate fluid balance, acid-base balance, nerve and muscle function, heart rhythm and other normal body processes. [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/lab-tests/electrolyte-panel/) describes these roles and notes that electrolytes are obtained from food and drinks.

Your body regulates their concentrations continuously. Drinking a glass of plain water does not somehow strip electrolytes from your body, and a normal day does not automatically require an electrolyte powder.

## Water or electrolytes? A quick guide

For a healthy adult, the situation usually matters more than the marketing on the bottle.

| Situation | A reasonable starting point |
| --- | --- |
| Normal day with regular meals | Water and ordinary food |
| Short or moderate everyday exercise | Water is usually sufficient |
| Prolonged exercise with heavy sweating | Electrolyte-containing fluids may be useful |
| Several hours of sweating in heat | Water plus replacement of lost electrolytes may be appropriate |
| Significant diarrhoea or vomiting | Oral rehydration solution may be appropriate |
| Mild thirst after sitting at a desk | Water |
| Kidney disease or medicines affecting potassium or fluid balance | Follow individual medical advice |

These are broad categories, not strict cut-offs. Sweat losses vary widely between people, activities and environments. The [National Athletic Trainers' Association](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5634236/) recommends individualising fluid replacement rather than relying on one universal formula.

## Do you need electrolytes every day?

Usually, you do not need a special electrolyte drink simply because electrolytes are important.

MedlinePlus notes that we obtain electrolytes from food and fluids. CDC/NIOSH guidance for people working in heat goes further: in general, regular meals and adequate water are sufficient to maintain water and electrolyte balance. [CDC/NIOSH](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/mining/UserFiles/works/products/training/keepingcool/NIOSHMiningFactSheets.pdf)

That is a useful distinction. An electrolyte drink can solve a real problem, but the problem is not "I drank plain water today".

Normal meals already provide sodium, potassium, magnesium and other minerals. The body then regulates their concentrations through the kidneys, hormones and fluid balance.

## What changes when you sweat?

Sweat contains water and electrolytes, particularly sodium and chloride.

The amount lost varies enormously. The National Athletic Trainers' Association reports large differences in both sweat rate and sodium loss between people and recommends basing replacement plans on individual circumstances when losses are substantial. [NATA position statement](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5634236/)

That is why two people doing the same activity may not need the same replacement strategy.

A relaxed indoor workout with modest sweating is very different from a long run in summer heat or several hours of physical work outdoors.

## When is plain water usually enough during exercise?

For most recreational activity, water remains a sensible default.

The NATA position statement says that most people can avoid fluid-balance problems by drinking when thirsty during and after exercise and eating a healthy diet. It also emphasises that fluid needs vary with body size, exercise intensity, environment, acclimatisation and individual sweat rate. [NATA](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5634236/)

This means there is no useful universal rule saying that an electrolyte drink becomes necessary at exactly 45, 60 or 90 minutes.

For a short gym session, walk, easy run or ordinary bike ride, especially in mild conditions, plain water is usually a reasonable choice.

As duration, heat and sweat losses increase, electrolyte replacement becomes more relevant.

## When do electrolytes become more useful?

CDC/NIOSH guidance provides a practical example for prolonged heat exposure. For moderate activity in heat lasting less than two hours, it recommends water. When sweating continues for several hours, it recommends sports drinks containing balanced electrolytes. [CDC/NIOSH Workplace Recommendations](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/heat-stress/recommendations/)

That does not mean "two hours" is a biological switch for everyone. It shows the general principle: the bigger and longer the sweat losses, the more relevant electrolyte replacement becomes.

Electrolytes may therefore be useful when you are:

- exercising for a long time;
- sweating heavily for several hours;
- doing repeated hard training sessions;
- working physically in significant heat;
- replacing a large fluid loss after prolonged exercise.

If you are simply having a warm day but are not sweating heavily, normal food and water may still be perfectly adequate.

For more detail on heat, see [How Much Water Should You Drink in Hot Weather?](/blog/how-much-water-should-you-drink-in-hot-weather/).

## Salty sweat does not give you an exact sodium requirement

Some people notice white marks on clothing, salt on the skin or a very salty taste after exercise.

That can be consistent with losing a noticeable amount of salt in sweat, but it does not tell you exactly how many milligrams of sodium you need to replace.

NATA data show that sweat sodium concentration varies greatly between individuals. That is one reason hydration strategies for endurance athletes or people with unusually high sweat losses may need to be personalised. [NATA](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5634236/)

For most occasional exercise, there is no need to turn every workout into a laboratory test. But if you repeatedly train for hours, have very high sweat losses or have previously had hydration problems, individual advice can be useful.

## Illness is a different kind of electrolyte problem

Vomiting and diarrhoea are not the same as sweating during exercise.

The [World Health Organization](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/diarrhoeal-disease) explains that diarrhoea causes losses of both water and electrolytes, including sodium, chloride, potassium and bicarbonate.

For dehydration caused by diarrhoea, WHO recommends oral rehydration solution, usually called ORS. ORS contains water, salts and glucose in a specific formulation that helps replace the fluid and electrolytes being lost. [WHO Oral Rehydration Salts](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/WHO-FCH-CAH-06.1)

This is why "electrolytes" is too broad a word on its own. The right product for a marathon is not automatically the right product for diarrhoeal illness.

Our guide [How to Stay Hydrated When You're Sick](/blog/how-to-stay-hydrated-when-sick/) covers vomiting, diarrhoea and ORS in more detail.

## Is a sports drink the same as oral rehydration solution?

No.

Both may contain water, carbohydrate and electrolytes, but their purposes and formulations are different.

Sports drinks are designed primarily for exercise, where fluid, sodium and sometimes carbohydrate replacement can be useful.

ORS is a medical rehydration formulation. WHO describes it as a glucose-electrolyte solution used to prevent or treat dehydration caused by diarrhoea. [WHO](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/WHO-FCH-CAH-06.1)

If someone has significant gastrointestinal fluid loss, a normal sports drink should not automatically be treated as an equivalent substitute for ORS.

## What about electrolyte powders and tablets?

The word "electrolyte" on the front of a packet tells you very little about what is inside it.

Products can differ substantially in:

- sodium;
- potassium;
- magnesium;
- carbohydrate;
- sweeteners;
- serving size.

One product may contain enough sodium to be aimed at prolonged endurance exercise. Another may provide only small quantities of several minerals and be designed mainly as a flavoured drink.

There is no standard rule that one tablet or sachet equals one correct "dose" of hydration.

If you are buying an electrolyte product for a specific reason, check the actual nutrition information rather than counting the number of minerals listed on the front.

## Why sodium matters during prolonged sweating

Electrolyte marketing often gives equal visual weight to sodium, potassium and magnesium, but they do not all play the same role in sweat replacement.

Sodium is a major electrolyte in extracellular fluid and is lost in sweat. In the NATA position statement, measured sodium losses during exercise vary widely, which is why prolonged and heavy sweating can make sodium replacement relevant. [NATA](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5634236/)

Magnesium and potassium are important nutrients too, but a drink is not automatically better for hydration because it contains a longer list of minerals.

The useful question is what you are trying to replace.

## Can you have too many electrolytes?

Yes. Electrolyte levels can become too high as well as too low.

[MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/fluidandelectrolytebalance.html) notes that electrolyte imbalances can occur with severe vomiting or diarrhoea, heavy sweating, kidney, liver or heart problems, medicines, dehydration and overhydration.

Potassium deserves particular caution. In healthy people with normal kidney function, excess potassium from ordinary food is usually removed in urine. But people with chronic kidney disease or those taking certain medicines can develop dangerously high potassium levels.

The [NIH Office of Dietary Supplements](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Potassium-Consumer/) specifically warns about ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers and potassium-sparing diuretics because they can raise blood potassium.

If you have been given medical advice about sodium, potassium or fluid intake, that advice takes priority over a general hydration guide or supplement label.

## Do muscle cramps prove that you need electrolytes?

No.

A muscle cramp is not an electrolyte test.

Cramps can occur for different reasons, so it is too simplistic to interpret every cramp as proof of sodium or magnesium deficiency.

If cramps repeatedly occur during long periods of heavy sweating, fluid and electrolyte loss may be one part of the situation. But taking an electrolyte supplement every time a muscle cramps is not a reliable way to diagnose the cause.

When a true electrolyte imbalance is suspected medically, an electrolyte panel is a blood test used to measure the body's main electrolytes. [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/lab-tests/electrolyte-panel/)

## What about electrolyte drinks after alcohol?

An electrolyte drink does not cancel the effects of alcohol or make heavy drinking safe.

If you can eat and drink normally, ordinary food and fluids provide water and electrolytes. If vomiting is substantial or you cannot keep fluids down, the problem is no longer a routine "hangover hydration" question and should be treated as ongoing fluid loss.

The same principle applies here as elsewhere in this article: the useful choice depends on what has actually been lost and what you are able to replace.

## A practical decision guide

For an otherwise healthy adult, this is a simple way to approach the choice.

### Choose water first when:

- you are having a normal day;
- you are mildly thirsty;
- you are doing short or moderate recreational activity;
- you are eating normally;
- you have not been sweating heavily for a prolonged period.

### Consider electrolyte-containing fluids when:

- exercise lasts a long time;
- sweating has been heavy for several hours;
- you are doing physical work or training in significant heat;
- you need to replace a substantial sweat loss.

### Think about ORS rather than an ordinary sports drink when:

- diarrhoea is causing significant fluid loss;
- vomiting or gastrointestinal illness is causing ongoing losses;
- a pharmacist or healthcare professional recommends oral rehydration.

### Get individual medical advice when:

- you have kidney disease;
- you have heart, liver or another condition affecting fluid balance;
- you take medicines that affect potassium, sodium or fluid balance;
- you have symptoms suggesting a significant electrolyte disturbance.

## What should you log in a hydration tracker?

An electrolyte drink still contains fluid, so it can be part of a hydration log. But a tracker cannot determine whether you needed the sodium or potassium inside that drink.

If you use [Octi Flow](https://octiflow.app/), the useful role of the app is to make your drinking pattern visible: what you drank, approximately how much and when. It should not turn an electrolyte product into a medical recommendation.

On an ordinary day, water can remain the default. When circumstances change because of prolonged exercise, heavy sweating, heat or illness, the type of fluid may matter more.

## What to remember

Electrolytes are essential. Special electrolyte drinks are not essential every day.

For most healthy adults eating regular meals, water is an appropriate default for everyday hydration and ordinary recreational activity. Electrolyte replacement becomes more useful when sweat losses are large and prolonged.

Illness is different. Significant diarrhoea or vomiting can require a rehydration strategy designed for gastrointestinal losses, which is why oral rehydration solution should not be confused with a standard sports drink.

Before reaching for an electrolyte product, ask a simple question: **what loss am I trying to replace?**

If there is no clear reason to replace additional salts, plain water may already be doing the job.
